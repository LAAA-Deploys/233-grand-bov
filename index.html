The Assignment

Prepared for the Matthew E Meyer Trust

You asked what the property is worth as it stands today, and what it is worth with the two long tenured units delivered vacant, because the gap between those figures decides whether it makes sense to spend money on tenant buyouts before a sale. This analysis answers that directly. It sets out two ways to bring the property to market, each with an asking price and the opinion of value beneath it, and identifies the buyout number at which the exercise stops paying for itself.

The property

233-239 S Grand Avenue is a four unit property built in 1927, comprising 1,848 square feet of building area across four one bedroom, one bathroom units averaging 462 square feet, plus four detached garages. The site is 4,497 square feet, zoned R2-1XL-CPIO, in the Central San Pedro submarket.

All four units are covered by the City of Los Angeles Rent Stabilization Ordinance and all four are month to month. Properties West manages the asset. The owner pays water and trash; tenants pay their own gas and electric.

Where the value sits

Two of the four units have been occupied since 2011 at $834 per month. The other two turned in 2023 and sit at $1,568 and $1,512, which ownership considers at or near market. That gap is the entire investment thesis, and it is also the entire valuation question.

The soft story seismic retrofit was completed and permitted in 2024 at a cost of $34,000. That work is done, and it is worth real money against comparable San Pedro buildings that still carry the obligation.

The mark to market is real but it is not free. Under Costa-Hawkins, a rent stabilized unit in Los Angeles resets to market on a voluntary vacancy and is then re-controlled going forward. That makes a negotiated buyout the practical lever on the two 2011 tenancies. Los Angeles buyouts run through the Housing Department Tenant Buyout Notification Program, which requires a disclosure form to the tenant, a signed agreement filed with the City, and a rescission window during which the tenant may withdraw.
Rent growth alone will not close the gap. The allowable annual increase under the ordinance was 3 percent for the year running July 2025 through June 2026. Across all four units that is $142 per month of additional rent for the year. The City 2026 solid waste fee increase was $35.20 per unit per month, or $141 per month across the building. One fee increase consumed essentially the entire year allowable rent increase. On an $834 unit the allowable increase is $25 per month against $35 of new cost, so those two units moved backward in real terms. As of February 2, 2026 the ordinance no longer permits any additional percentage increase for utilities, which closed the one mechanism that previously offset this.

Renter demand

At 67.4 percent renter occupied, 90731 is more rental dependent than either the City or the County. Median gross rent across all unit types in the zip is $1,740, which sits above the $1,600 projected market rent used in this analysis for a 462 square foot one bedroom. That supports the projection as reasonable rather than aggressive.

Opportunity Zone

The parcel sits within a federal Qualified Opportunity Zone, which may be relevant to a buyer structuring around capital gains deferral. One note of caution: the Opportunity Zone tract reference and the 2020 Census tract reference in the public parcel record do not match one another, so eligibility should be confirmed independently before anyone relies on it.

Zoning note. The site is zoned R2-1XL with a Community Plan Implementation Overlay. R2 permits one unit per 2,000 square feet of lot area, which on a 4,497 square foot lot allows two units. The existing four units therefore represent legal nonconforming density: the building cannot be reproduced at its current unit count under today code. This protects existing income but forecloses a straightforward redevelopment argument, and it is worth confirming with the City before a buyer lender or insurer raises it.

How this set was chosen, and what was left out. The comparables were selected on unit mix, which is the primary value discriminator for two to four unit product bought on residential financing. The subject is four one bedroom units, so the set carries the meaningful one bedroom and studio content in the cohort. Twelve further San Pedro fourplex sales from the same export were reviewed and excluded as two and three bedroom product: 244 W Sepulveda, 1431 S Gaffey, 902 S Mesa, 464 W 17th, 358 W 17th, 841 W 5th, 789 W 18th, 1012 W 18th, 1085 W 19th, 994 W 7th, 663 W 2nd and 719 Oro Terrace, ranging from $875,000 to $1,625,000 and $223 to $400 per square foot.

The comparables in brief

A. 359 W Amar St, half a mile away, is the closest match in the set. Four one bedroom units at 399 square feet, built 1918, LAR2 zoning, and the same 13-245 tax rate area as the subject. One unit was vacant at close. It had earlier been listed at $800,000 and cancelled before selling at $650,000. It is one half of a side by side eight unit complex, so a buyer could take either a commercial loan or two residential loans.

B. 226 N Western Ave is two studios, a one bedroom and a three bedroom house on one lot. Post war 1964 construction at 822 square feet per unit, which is why it prices well below the pre-1930 comparables per square foot. One studio was remodelled and delivered vacant. 57 days on market.

C. 1025 W 12th St is 1924 vintage across two buildings with alley access, three one bedroom units around 653 square feet plus a rear three bedroom at $3,465. Sold out of an Invitation Homes entity having last traded at $541,000 in 2013 out of REO.

D. 1260 W 9th St is the renovated condition indicator. It was acquired for $532,000 earlier in 2025 and resold at $1,280,000 in November, so the price reflects a completed repositioning rather than in place condition. Two turnkey two bedroom units with high end finishes, four private garages, and cited ADU capacity.

What the set says about the subject

On market rents the subject sits inside the range. On current rents it sits above all of it. That gap is the deal. At the recommended list price the gross rent multiplier on market rents is 11.07, comfortably within the comparable range of 7.52 to 12.26. On current rents it is 14.92, above every comparable in the set. Nothing about the building explains that spread. It is entirely the two units at $834.

On a per unit basis the subject is below three of the four comparables and 13.8 percent below the set average of $246,625. On a per bedroom basis it is above only comparable A. Both measures support the asking price.

Per square foot runs the other way, and it should be stated rather than buried. At 1,848 square feet the subject is the smallest building in the set by a wide margin, and small buildings always carry a higher price per square foot. The measure is least reliable precisely where unit counts match but building sizes do not, which is why the set is weighed on unit mix and per unit pricing instead.

The constraint worth naming: comparable A. 359 W Amar is the closest analog on every physical dimension, and it closed at $650,000 with a current gross of $71,340, roughly 25 percent more income than the subject produces today. A buyer who anchors on that sale will start well below the asking price, and the honest answer to them is the rent roll rather than the building: A was already collecting close to market across all four units, so it had no loss to lease left to buy. The subject has $19,824 of it. That is what the pricing gap between the two properties represents, and it is the argument the marketing has to win.

What is not carried above, and why it still matters. This underwriting is presented on an owner managed basis. A four unit property qualifies for residential financing, which is underwritten on the borrower personal income rather than on property debt coverage, and buyers at this size commonly self manage. Property management is therefore excluded, though the current owner pays $3,379 a year for it. Trash and reserves are likewise not carried as line items. A buyer who intends to use third party management should add back roughly $3,300 for management, $2,857 for trash, and $1,000 for reserves, about $7,200 in total, which would put net operating income near $27,100.
A note on trash, because it is a moving number. The City of Los Angeles solid waste fee for buildings of four units or fewer rose to $59.53 per unit per month in 2026, a 130 percent increase for three and four unit properties, and it is scheduled to reach $65.93 by 2030. It is billed on the LADWP account, so it can be easy to miss inside a water line. For this building that is roughly $2,857 a year today. It cannot be passed through to tenants: the rent stabilization ordinance permits only the RSO registration and SCEP fee surcharges, about $4.44 per unit per month combined, and as of February 2, 2026 no additional utility percentage increase is allowed. Whoever owns this building absorbs the fee, and it rises annually.

As is net operating income and cap rate are shown at the $765,000 opinion floor. The delivered vacant figures hold units 233 and 237 at their current rents and mark 235 and 239 to the $1,600 projected market rent, with net operating income and cap rate shown at the $950,000 list price.

The argument for the as is price is the loss to lease, and only the loss to lease. At $850,000 the gross rent multiplier on current rents is 14.92, above every comparable in the set. On market rents it is 11.07, comfortably inside the range of 7.52 to 12.26. Nothing about the building explains that spread. It is entirely the two units at $834, which carry $19,824 a year of loss to lease against a $76,800 market gross. A buyer at this price is buying that gap, and the marketing has to say so directly rather than hoping the per unit number carries it alone.
Why the ask sits above the opinion of value, and the caution that comes with it. An asking price is a marketing decision, not a prediction. It is set to bring qualified buyers to the table and to leave room for the negotiation that follows. The comparable set carries one direct warning about how far that can be pushed. 359 W Amar, the closest analog to this property, was first listed at $800,000, cancelled without selling, and eventually traded at $650,000, and it was collecting roughly 25 percent more income than the subject does today. An eight hundreds asking price on a San Pedro fourplex is therefore not automatically credible to this buyer pool. A negotiation of 6.5 percent from $850,000 lands at $794,750, the top of the opinion of value, and 10 percent lands on $765,000, the floor.

The recommended next step

Before spending anything, have a conversation with the tenants in 235 and 239 and simply ask what it would take for them to move. That costs nothing and it converts the entire question from an estimate into a fact.

If either is willing to move for something in the region of $20,000, the economics work comfortably. If they open materially above $30,000, or if the conversation suggests they have no intention of leaving at any reasonable number, then reconsider: at that point the buyout consumes the value it was meant to create, and selling as is becomes the better outcome.

Any buyout must run through the Housing Department Tenant Buyout Notification Program, with a disclosure form served on the tenant, a signed agreement filed with the City, and a rescission window during which the tenant may withdraw. Both tenancies are at roughly fifteen years, which places them in the highest relocation tier and will inform what they think their position is worth.

What to expect on timing

226 N Western took 57 days on market. More instructive is 359 W Amar, which was listed at $800,000, cancelled, and later closed at $650,000. Neither outcome is distressed; both are what this submarket looks like. Either asking price should be carried with the expectation of a real marketing period and at least one price conversation, not a quick clearing.

Why a full renovation is not a third option

Taking the other two units to $1,600 as well would lift gross scheduled rent from $75,360 to $76,800, a difference of $1,440 a year, because those two are already close to market. The exterior stucco, the windows, the electrical and the sewer work would improve the building without materially improving the income, and the price per square foot is already the weakest measure in the set. 1260 W 9th St shows a full repositioning can move a number in this submarket, but it delivered two turnkey two bedroom units with high end finishes, four private garages and cited ADU capacity. Refreshing four 462 square foot one bedrooms is not that project.

Where $950,000 sits, stated honestly. With both units delivered vacant the price per unit of $237,500 is 3.7 percent below the set average and below two of the four comparables, which supports it. The gross rent multiplier of 12.61 sits just above the top of the comparable range of 12.49, and that top figure belongs to 1260 W 9th St, a completed renovation with turnkey finishes. Asking a marginally higher multiple than a renovated comparable, for a building that has been made vacant but not renovated, is the one place this pricing leans forward. It is defensible on the per unit measure and on the strength of delivering two units at market, but it should be carried knowingly.
A word on precision. Valuation of a four unit building in a thin submarket is an art rather than an exact science. Four comparable sales is a real evidence base but it is not a large one, and reasonable people will land on different numbers from the same data. These figures are pricing strategy as much as they are valuation: a list price is chosen to bring qualified buyers to the table and to create the conditions for a competitive negotiation, not to predict a closing price to the dollar. Expect the market to have opinions, and expect the final number to be settled by what a buyer will actually sign.
One offsetting positive not reflected in the comparables. The $34,000 soft story seismic retrofit is complete and permitted. Buildings of this vintage in San Pedro that have not completed the work carry both the cost and the compliance risk. This should be presented as a specific credit against any comparable that has not retrofitted.
