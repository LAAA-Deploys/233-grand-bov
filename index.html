Buyer Profile & Transaction Strategy
Who Is Most Likely to Buy, and How We Create Competition

Market Context

A four unit building finances as residential rather than commercial. A buyer qualifies on personal income with roughly 75% of the rents credited, and the property is not underwritten to a debt service coverage test. That single fact governs who bids and what they pay. Owner users compete directly with investors, and small San Pedro fourplexes consequently clear at going in yields well below what a commercial multifamily buyer would accept.

It also shapes the comparable evidence. One of the four recorded sales in this set went to an owner occupant at $320,000 per unit, the highest price per unit in the set, following a purchase earlier the same year at $532,000. That is a different buyer with a different arithmetic, and this opinion weights it accordingly.

Positioning Thesis

The building will be marketed to two distinct groups, and the material differs for each. An owner user cares about the vacant unit path, the condition of the two turned units, and the monthly carry after financing. A private investor cares about the loss to lease, the cost and probability of a buyout, and the deferred maintenance schedule.

For both, the honest position is that this is a well located, structurally sound vintage fourplex with a large and genuine rent gap that is expensive and uncertain to capture. Presenting it as a straightforward value add would not survive the first conversation about the Rent Stabilization Ordinance. Presenting it as what it is, with the buyout arithmetic in the buyer's hands, reaches the buyer actually equipped to execute.

Likely Buyer Profiles

Likely Buyer 01

The local owner user

Buys with residential financing, occupies one unit, and treats the other three as carry. Values the completed seismic work and the garages, and is the least sensitive to cap rate of the three. This is the buyer who paid $320,000 per unit two blocks west.

Likely Buyer 02

The private San Pedro investor

Owns two to ten small buildings in the harbor area, self manages, and underwrites without a management fee. Prices deferred maintenance carefully and will discount for the original windows and the electrical work.

Likely Buyer 03

The patient rent gap buyer

Underwrites the two long held units at market and accepts that turnover may take years. Pays for the optionality rather than for a contracted path, and is the buyer most likely to move if a buyout is signed before or during escrow.

233-239 S Grand Avenue is a four unit building of 1927 vintage on the east side of Grand, four blocks from the harbor and a short walk from downtown San Pedro. The improvements run 1,848 square feet across four one bedroom units averaging 462 square feet, with four detached garages behind. The ownership pays water and sewer. Tenants carry their own gas and electricity.

The building's economics are defined by two tenancies. Units 233 and 237 turned in 2023 and rent for $1,568 and $1,512. Units 235 and 239 have been occupied since 2011 and rent for $834 each. A remodeled one bedroom three blocks away leased at $1,800 in January, so the two long held units sit at roughly 46% of what the same product achieves today. That gap is the whole of the value question here, and because both units are covered by the Rent Stabilization Ordinance it cannot be closed by raising rent.

This opinion answers the two questions the ownership asked. What is the property worth as it stands, and what is it worth with units 235 and 239 delivered vacant. The Buyout Scenarios section then sets out the arithmetic connecting the two, with every assumption adjustable.

Location Overview
Central San Pedro

San Pedro sits at the southern end of Los Angeles, wrapped around the Port of Los Angeles and reached by the 110 Freeway. The 90731 zip carries a population near 62,000, a median household income of $76,395 and a median gross rent of $1,740. Renters occupy 67.4% of households, above the 64.0% figure for the City of Los Angeles and the 54.1% figure for the County.

The subject sits within walking distance of the 6th Street and 7th Street corridors and the waterfront redevelopment at the foot of the hill. Small vintage income property dominates the immediate blocks, which is what makes the comparable set for a building of this size and age unusually tight.

The improvements comprise two structures totalling 1,848 square feet, arranged as four one bedroom, one bathroom units of roughly 462 square feet each, with four detached garages at the rear. Construction is wood frame on a raised foundation, built in 1927. The parcel runs 4,497 square feet and is zoned R2-1XL within a Community Plan Implementation Overlay.

The seismic work is complete and on the record. LADBS shows sill plate anchor bolts and cripple wall bracing to City Standard Plan 1 under Chapter 92, together with replacement of 30 damaged pier pads and posts, all finaled on 11 April 2024. The ownership reports the cost at $34,000. One clarification matters for how that work is valued. The building is not enrolled in the Chapter 93 mandatory soft story program, which applies to multi storey buildings with tuck under parking rather than to single storey wood frame construction of this type. The work performed is real, permitted and finaled, and it removes a genuine cost from a buyer's plan. It is not a soft story retrofit, and comparable San Pedro buildings of this size do not carry a soft story obligation that this property has discharged.

The ownership describes the building as undermaintained with significant deferred maintenance. Exterior stucco and paint are due, the original windows remain throughout and need replacement, and electrical work is required. A sewer scope and a termite inspection are recommended and have not been performed. Those items are priced into the opinion of value rather than carried as an operating expense.

The property is underwritten on the residential basis that applies to a two to four unit building. Income is actual scheduled rent with no vacancy factor. Four recorded fourplex sales in San Pedro between November 2025 and February 2026 frame the value. They run from $162,500 to $320,000 per unit and from $263 to $417 per square foot, and that spread is explained almost entirely by unit size. The set runs from 399 to 916 square feet per unit against the subject's 462. Only 359 W Amar Street brackets this building on size and vintage, and it is the most instructive sale in the set for a further reason. It carried in place rent 25% higher than the subject's, it was listed at $800,000, that price was withdrawn, and it sold in December at $650,000.

Applied to the subject, the gross rent multiplier evidence indicates $519,000 to $712,000 with a median near $686,000, and the price per square foot evidence indicates $487,000 to $770,000 with a median near $699,000. Price per unit is not usable here without adjustment, because unit sizes across the set vary by a factor of 2.3. Weighting 359 W Amar most heavily for comparability, crediting the completed seismic work and the four garages, and deducting for the original windows, stucco and electrical work, the opinion of value as the property stands today is $680,000 to $750,000. The recommended asking price is $775,000, which allows a negotiation into the range.

With units 235 and 239 delivered vacant and renovated to the standard the 666 W 7th lease describes, scheduled rent rises to $80,160 and net operating income to roughly $59,900. On the same evidence the opinion of value in that condition is $835,000 to $925,000, and the recommended asking price is $950,000. Buyout Scenarios
233-239 S Grand

Units 235 and 239 have been occupied since 2011 at $834 a month, against a renovated market rent near $1,800 demonstrated by a signed lease three blocks away. Both units are covered by the Rent Stabilization Ordinance, so reaching that rent means a voluntary buyout, a renovation, and a re-lease. This section lays out that arithmetic and lets you move every assumption in it.

What it returns is a ceiling rather than a recommendation: the most that could be paid to a tenant while still earning the profit margin you set. It assumes both units are turned and it does not assume either tenant accepts. Whether to pursue a buyout at all is your decision, and it turns on facts about each household that only you and the tenants know.
