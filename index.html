The Assignment

Prepared for the Matthew E Meyer Trust

You asked for two numbers: what the property is worth as it stands today, and what it is worth with the two long tenured units delivered vacant. The spread between those figures is what decides whether it makes sense to spend money on tenant buyouts before a sale. This analysis answers that question directly, and shows the work behind both numbers.

The property

233-239 S Grand Avenue is a four unit property built in 1927, comprising 1,848 square feet of building area across four one bedroom, one bathroom units averaging 462 square feet, plus four detached garages. The site is 4,356 square feet, zoned R2-1XL-CPIO, in the Central San Pedro submarket.

All four units are covered by the City of Los Angeles Rent Stabilization Ordinance and all four are month to month. Properties West manages the asset. The owner pays water and trash; tenants pay their own gas and electric.

Where the value sits

Two of the four units have been occupied since 2011 at $834 per month. The other two turned in 2023 and sit at $1,568 and $1,512, which ownership considers at or near market. That gap is the entire investment thesis, and it is also the entire valuation question.

The soft story seismic retrofit was completed and permitted in 2024 at a cost of $34,000. That work is done, and it is worth real money against comparable San Pedro buildings that still carry the obligation.

The mark to market is real but it is not free. Under Costa-Hawkins, a rent stabilized unit in Los Angeles resets to market on a voluntary vacancy and is then re-controlled going forward. That makes a negotiated buyout the practical lever on the two 2011 tenancies. Los Angeles buyouts run through the Housing Department Tenant Buyout Notification Program, which requires a disclosure form to the tenant, a signed agreement filed with the City, and a rescission window during which the tenant may withdraw.
Rent growth alone will not close the gap. The allowable annual increase under the ordinance was 3 percent for the year running July 2025 through June 2026. Across all four units that is $142 per month of additional rent for the year. The City 2026 solid waste fee increase was $35.20 per unit per month, or $141 per month across the building. One fee increase consumed essentially the entire year allowable rent increase. On an $834 unit the allowable increase is $25 per month against $35 of new cost, so those two units moved backward in real terms. As of February 2, 2026 the ordinance no longer permits any additional percentage increase for utilities, which closed the one mechanism that previously offset this.

Renter demand

At 67.4 percent renter occupied, 90731 is more rental dependent than either the City or the County. Median gross rent across all unit types in the zip is $1,740, which sits above the $1,600 projected market rent used in this analysis for a 462 square foot one bedroom. That supports the projection as reasonable rather than aggressive.

Opportunity Zone

The parcel sits within a federal Qualified Opportunity Zone, which may be relevant to a buyer structuring around capital gains deferral. One note of caution: the Opportunity Zone tract reference and the 2020 Census tract reference in the public parcel record do not match one another, so eligibility should be confirmed independently before anyone relies on it.

Zoning note. The site is zoned R2-1XL with a Community Plan Implementation Overlay. R2 permits one unit per 2,000 square feet of lot area, which on a 4,356 square foot lot allows two units. The existing four units therefore represent legal nonconforming density: the building cannot be reproduced at its current unit count under today code. This protects existing income but forecloses a straightforward redevelopment argument, and it is worth confirming with the City before a buyer lender or insurer raises it.

What the comparables actually show

Price per unit tracks unit size and vintage, not in place income. At 518 square feet per unit the market paid $222,000; at 800 square feet, $226,250; at 1,063 square feet, $237,500. That is a consistent progression of roughly $28 for each additional square foot of unit size.

The clearest evidence that income is not driving price per unit: 1431 S Gaffey had lower net operating income per unit than 2023 S Crescent, yet sold for more per unit, and at a 4.00 percent cap rate against the Crescent 5.00 percent. Asset quality and unit size set the price per unit. In place income sets the cap rate a buyer will accept.

This is consistent with how four unit properties are financed. One to four units qualifies for residential financing, underwritten on the borrower personal income rather than on property debt coverage, which is why these assets price closer to housing than to commercial real estate.

Direction of the market

The December 2023 sale at $260,000 per unit sits well above the 2025 transactions, which averaged $228,583, a decline of roughly 12 percent. Within 2025 the market stabilised rather than continued falling: June closed at $222,000 and $226,250 per unit, and October at $237,500.

An independent corroboration of that correction: 577 W 4th St carried an assessed value of $1,150,000 at the time of sale but traded at $905,000 after a fifteen month hold, implying the prior owner absorbed a loss of roughly 21 percent.

Pricing and timing evidence

2023 S Crescent was marketed at $950,000 and closed at $888,000, a reduction of 6.5 percent, with deferred maintenance cited as the reason. 577 W 4th St required 167 days on market. The subject deferred maintenance is more extensive than the Crescent, and both data points should inform the list strategy and the expected timeline.

Where the subject sits. At 462 square feet per unit the subject is the smallest product in the set and, at 1927, among the oldest. Its in place rent of $1,187 per unit per month is 17.2 percent below the $1,434 implied at 2023 S Crescent. On unit size, vintage, condition, and income the subject belongs at or below the bottom of the observed range.

Does it pencil?

Both 2011 tenancies are at roughly fifteen years, which places them in the highest relocation tier under the City schedule and anchors any negotiation accordingly. A realistic all in cost is $25,000 to $50,000 per unit in buyout, plus $15,000 to $25,000 per unit of renovation to actually achieve $1,600 on a 462 square foot unit with original windows. Call it $80,000 to $150,000 for both.

Against a spread of $145,000 to $205,000, the buyouts pencil. But the margin is real rather than overwhelming, and it is genuinely sensitive to what the buyouts cost. At the pessimistic end of buyout cost and the conservative end of the spread, the exercise roughly breaks even.

How to read that

The honest conclusion is that this is a judgment call rather than an obvious one. If both tenants can be bought out at the lower end of the range, the economics are clearly favourable. If either negotiation runs long or expensive, the advantage narrows quickly.

There is also a sequencing consideration. Buyouts take time, renovation takes more, and the submarket declined roughly 12 percent between the end of 2023 and mid 2025 before stabilising. A twelve to eighteen month value creation programme carries market risk that a sale today does not.

A middle path worth considering: market the property as is, disclose the upside clearly, and let a buyer price it. The comparables suggest buyers in this submarket are already paying for that upside themselves.

Why $745,000 rather than a higher number. On price per unit the subject sits 16.1 percent below the closest analog, 2023 S Crescent, which is justified: the subject units are 462 square feet against the Crescent 518, its in place income is 17.2 percent lower, its deferred maintenance is more extensive, and it carries owner paid water and trash where the comparables are individually metered. On price per land square foot, $171.03 sits at the very bottom of the observed $170.54 to $185.32 range. On price per building square foot, $403.14 sits just under the Crescent $428.99. The number is supported on three independent measures.
One offsetting positive not reflected in the comparables. The $34,000 soft story seismic retrofit is complete and permitted. Buildings of this vintage in San Pedro that have not completed the work carry both the cost and the compliance risk. This should be presented as a specific credit against any comparable that has not retrofitted.
